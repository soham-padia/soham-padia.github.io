GLU Gate Lab

A transformer's feed-forward layer has a gate: a small function that decides how much of each hidden unit gets through. Most models today use SwiGLU. I trained a small model (10 layers, d_model 512) with 21 other gates: staircases meant to make the layer cheaper to quantize or skip, and a few that borrow the rules of Conway's Game of Life. Five seeds each, 172 runs in all. On loss nothing beat SwiGLU, and putting the exact Game of Life rule on top of it tied.

Every gate in the study sits in the same spot of the feed-forward block. The layer computes two projections of the token, bends one of them with a gate, multiplies, and projects back. Pick a gate to see its shape, the slope training sees, and what it does to a live layer of hidden units.

A live layer

64 hidden units of one layer. Each new token draws fresh pre-activations a and b; the selected gate turns them into g and h = g · u. Exact zeros and near-zero gates are what a sparse kernel could skip. Hover a unit for its numbers.

The neighbour rule

This gate is pointwise: each unit sees only its own a. The life gates are different. They arrange the units on a ring (or, for conway2d_swiglu, a 2-D grid) and let each unit's gate depend on its 8 nearest neighbours, the way a cell in Conway's Game of Life looks at the 8 cells around it. Round 2 made the rule smooth; round 3 kept it exact and used it to scale SwiGLU.

Every gate here is a line-for-line port of the study's training code, checked against PyTorch's own outputs for all 22 gates on 1,320 layers of 64 units at three settings (largest relative gap 1.4e-6). The study numbers come from the 172 runs of rounds 1–3.

Notation: σ is the logistic sigmoid, r(x) = Δ/2π · sin(2πx/Δ), bell(x; c, w) = exp(−((x − c)/w)²).
